## 1.3 Use of estimates and judgments

The preparation of the interim condensed standalone financial statements in conformity with Ind AS requires management to make estimates, judgments, and assumptions. These affect the application of accounting policies; the reported amounts of assets and liabilities; disclosures of contingent assets and liabilities; and reported revenues and expenses during the period.

Critical accounting estimates involving complex and subjective judgments are disclosed in Note 1.4. Estimates may change as circumstances change. Actual results could differ from estimates. Changes in estimates and judgments are recognized in the period in which they are made, with material effects disclosed in the notes.

## 1.4 Critical accounting estimates and judgments

### a. Revenue recognition

The Company's customer contracts may include promises to transfer multiple products and services. Revenue is considered for recognition and measurement when:

- the contract has been approved in writing by the parties;
- the parties are committed to performing their respective obligations; and
- the contract is legally enforceable.

The Company assesses the services promised and identifies distinct performance obligations. Determining distinct deliverables, whether the customer can benefit independently from those deliverables, and allocating the transaction price to performance obligations involves significant judgment.

Fixed-price maintenance revenue is recognized ratably on a straight-line basis when services are performed through an indefinite number of repetitive acts over a specified period. When the pattern of benefits and the Company's fulfillment costs are uneven because services are discrete and non-repetitive, revenue is recognized ratably using a percentage-of-completion method. The method used depends on the contractual promises and the nature of the deliverables.

For other fixed-price contracts, the Company uses the percentage-of-completion method. Progress is measured using actual efforts or costs incurred to date as a proportion of estimated total efforts or costs. Estimating total efforts or costs involves significant judgment and is reassessed throughout the contract period using the latest available information.

Some integrated services arrangements include subcontractor services or third-party vendor equipment or software. Revenue from third-party products or services is recorded net of costs when the Company acts as an agent and gross when it acts as principal. The Company evaluates whether it obtains control of the specified goods or services before transfer to the customer. Factors considered include responsibility for fulfilling the promise, inventory risk, pricing discretion, and other indicators of control.

Provisions for estimated losses on incomplete contracts are recorded when such losses become probable, based on estimated efforts or costs to complete the contract.

### b. Income taxes

The Company's two major tax jurisdictions are India and the United States, although it also files tax returns in other overseas jurisdictions.

Significant judgment is involved in determining income tax provisions, including amounts expected to be paid or recovered for uncertain tax positions.

When assessing the realizability of deferred income tax assets, management considers whether some or all of those assets may not be realized. Ultimate realization depends on generating future taxable income during the periods in which temporary differences become deductible. Management considers scheduled reversals of deferred income tax liabilities, projected future taxable income, and tax-planning strategies.

Based on historical taxable income and projections for future taxable income during the periods in which deferred income tax assets are deductible, management believes the Company will realize the benefits of those deductible differences. The realizable amount could be reduced in the near term if estimates of future taxable income during the carry-forward period decline. Refer to Note 2.16.

### c. Property, plant and equipment

Property, plant and equipment represent a significant proportion of the Company's asset base. Periodic depreciation is based on estimated useful lives and expected residual values. Management determines useful lives and residual values when assets are acquired and reviews them periodically, including at each financial year-end.

Useful lives are based on historical experience with similar assets and anticipated future events, including changes in technology. Refer to Note 2.1.

## 2. Notes to the Interim Condensed Standalone Financial Statements

## 2.1 PROPERTY, PLANT AND EQUIPMENT

### Accounting Policy

Property, plant and equipment are stated at cost less accumulated depreciation and impairment, if any. Costs directly attributable to acquisition are capitalized until the assets are ready for use as intended by management.

Depreciation is calculated using the straight-line method over estimated useful lives.

The estimated useful lives of assets are as follows:

| Asset | Estimated useful life |
|---|---|
| Building (1) | 22–25 years |
| Plant and machinery (1) | 5 years |
| Office equipment | 5 years |
| Computer equipment (1) | 3–5 years |
| Furniture and fixtures (1) | 5 years |
| Vehicles (1) | 5 years |
| Leasehold improvements | Lower of the useful life of the asset or the lease term |

(1) Based on technical evaluation, management believes that the useful lives above best represent the periods over which it expects to use these assets. Accordingly, the useful lives for these assets differ from those prescribed under Part C of Schedule II of the Companies Act, 2013.

Depreciation methods, useful lives, and residual values are reviewed periodically, including at each financial year-end. Useful lives are based on historical experience with similar assets and anticipated future events, including changes in technology.

Advances paid toward the acquisition of property, plant and equipment outstanding at each Balance Sheet date are classified as capital advances under other non-current assets. The cost of assets not ready for use at that date is disclosed under “Capital work-in-progress.”

Subsequent expenditure relating to property, plant and equipment is capitalized only when future economic benefits are probable and the cost can be measured reliably. The cost and related accumulated depreciation are removed from the financial statements upon sale or retirement of the asset.

### Impairment

Property, plant and equipment are evaluated for recoverability whenever events or changes in circumstances indicate that their carrying amounts may not be recoverable. For impairment testing, the recoverable amount—the higher of fair value less costs to sell and value in use—is determined on an individual asset basis unless the asset does not generate cash flows that are largely independent of those from other assets. In such cases, the recoverable amount is determined for the Cash Generating Unit (CGU) to which the asset belongs.

If an asset is impaired, the impairment recognized in the interim condensed Statement of Profit and Loss equals the excess of its carrying value over its estimated recoverable amount. An impairment loss is reversed in the condensed Statement of Profit and Loss when there has been a change in the estimates used to determine the recoverable amount. The asset's carrying amount is increased to its revised recoverable amount, provided that this does not exceed the carrying amount that would have been determined, net of accumulated depreciation, had no impairment loss been recognized in prior years.
