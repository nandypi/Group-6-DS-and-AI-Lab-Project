## Our net income would decrease if the Government of India reduces or withdraws tax benefits and other incentives it provides to us.

Many of our development centers in India are registered as Special Economic Zone units (“SEZ units”) under the Special Economic Zone Act, 2005 (“SEZ Act”).

Under Section 10AA of the Income Tax Act, 1961, SEZ units that began providing services on or after April 1, 2005, are eligible for:

- A 100% income tax deduction on profits or gains derived from the export of services for the first five years beginning with the assessment year relevant to the previous year in which the SEZ unit begins providing services.
- A 50% deduction on those profits or gains for the following five years.
- Up to a 50% deduction for a further five years, subject to the creation of a Special Economic Zone Re-investment Reserve from the profits of eligible SEZ units and the use of that reserve to acquire new plant and machinery for the business, as permitted under the Income Tax Act.

If Infosys cannot use the SEZ Re-investment Reserve to invest in plant and machinery within the period specified under the Income Tax Act, it will have to pay tax on the unused reserve after the specified period expires. This would increase its effective tax rate.

## In the event that the Government of India or the Government of another country changes its tax policies in a manner that is adverse to us, our tax expense may materially increase, reducing our profitability.

India’s General Anti-Avoidance Rules (“GAAR”), introduced in connection with the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development’s (“OECD”) Base Erosion and Profit Shifting project, became applicable from fiscal 2018.

Under GAAR, an arrangement may be declared an “impermissible avoidance arrangement” if its main purpose, or one of its main purposes, is to obtain a tax benefit and it satisfies at least one of these tests:

- It creates rights and obligations that are not normally created between parties dealing at arm’s length.
- It results in misuse or abuse of tax-law provisions.
- It lacks commercial substance or is deemed to lack commercial substance.
- It is carried out in a manner not normally employed for a bona fide purpose.

If any Infosys transaction is found to be an impermissible avoidance arrangement under GAAR, its business, financial condition, and results of operations could be adversely affected.

The Finance Act, 2023, effective April 1, 2023, increased the withholding tax rate on payments to non-residents for royalties and/or fees for technical services from 10% to 20%, plus applicable surcharge and cess. The rate is subject to furnishing an Indian Permanent Account Number (“PAN”) or alternative documents where PAN is unavailable. A lower rate may apply where a Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement, read with the Multilateral Instrument (“MLI”), exists.

Based on a Supreme Court ruling, payments to non-residents for software purchases are not taxable as royalties when the payments are not characterized as royalties under an applicable Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement. Such payments are not subject to withholding where the relevant tax documents are furnished by the non-residents.

Infosys procures software licenses and technical services from non-residents in delivering products and services to clients. If relevant tax documents are not furnished to obtain Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement benefits, Infosys may have to gross up for withholding taxes, making the withholding tax an additional cost.

Infosys has entered into Advance Pricing Agreements (“APAs”) in multiple jurisdictions to provide greater predictability regarding tax obligations for overseas operations. Material changes to the critical assumptions underlying these APAs may affect taxes. When APAs expire or are under renewal, there is no certainty that they will be renewed or that renewal terms will be the same or similar.

Infosys operates in various countries, and changes in tax rates or tax laws in any country could affect its taxes. Changes in international tax laws and practices arising from the OECD Pillars of the Inclusive Framework on Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (“BEPS”), including global minimum tax and taxes on digital services, may affect tax costs.

## We operate in jurisdictions that impose transfer pricing and other tax-related regulations on us, and any failure to comply could adversely affect our profitability.

Infosys is required to comply with transfer pricing regulations in India and other applicable countries. It may also be required to comply with local and municipal tax regimes in several jurisdictions where it operates.

Failure to comply could result in additional taxes, penalties, and enforcement actions, adversely affecting profitability.

Changes in Indian government policies or political instability could adversely affect economic conditions in India and Infosys’ business and prospects. The Government of India could change laws and policies affecting technology companies, foreign investment, currency exchange, and investment in Infosys’ securities.

Infosys depends on the Reserve Bank of India (“RBI”) to pay foreign-exchange expenses and dividends. An exchange-control regime that restricts the ability to remit funds could affect its ability to deliver services and pay dividends. Political instability could delay reforms, adversely affect the market for securities of Indian companies—including Infosys’ equity shares and ADSs—and affect the market for its services.

## Attempts to fully address concerns of activist shareholders may divert the time and attention of our management and Board of Directors and may impact the prices of our equity shares and ADSs.

Responding to activist shareholder queries and concerns in a timely manner and to their full satisfaction may divert the attention of the Board and management and require significant costs.

Shareholder interactions may also affect Infosys’ reputation, client and investor sentiment, and the prices of its equity shares and ADSs.

## Our international expansion plans subject us to risks inherent to doing business internationally.

Because of its global presence, Infosys is subject to risks related to international expansion, including compliance with:

- Treaties and national and local laws.
- Multiple and potentially overlapping tax regimes.
- Privacy and data-protection laws.
- Export-control laws and restrictions on importing or exporting certain technologies.
- National and local labor laws concerning immigration, employee health and safety, wages, and benefits.

Infosys may face litigation or administrative actions brought by current or former employees, individually or as part of a class action, involving claims such as:

- Wrongful termination.
- Discrimination based on nationality, ethnicity, race, faith, gender, marital status, age, or disability.
- Misclassification.
- Redundancy payments under Transfer of Undertakings–Protection of Employment (“TUPE”)-type legislation.
- Other alleged labor-law violations or conduct.

If Infosys is held liable for unpaid compensation, redundancy payments, statutory penalties, or other damages arising from these actions and litigations, operating profitability could be adversely affected.

## Our ability to acquire companies organized outside India or set up step down subsidiaries in India may depend on the approval of the RBI, the Government of India and other regulatory approvals and failure to obtain this approval could negatively impact our business.

The RBI permits acquisitions of companies organized outside India by an Indian party under the automatic route, without approval, if applicable conditions are met. These include:

- The transaction consideration is paid in cash.
- The transaction value does not exceed 400% of the acquiring company’s net worth as of the date of its latest audited balance sheet.
- The acquisition is funded with cash from the acquiring company’s existing foreign-currency accounts or with cash proceeds from issuing ADRs or GDRs.
- The proposed acquisition structure falls within the permitted list.

Any financial commitment exceeding $1 billion or its equivalent in a financial year, or certain acquisition structures, requires prior RBI approval under the approval route, even when the total financial commitment remains within 400% of the acquiring company’s net worth based on its latest audited balance sheet.

RBI approval is also required to establish step-down Indian subsidiaries.

Failure to obtain required approval from the RBI or another government agency, including overseas regulatory approval for acquisitions outside India, could restrict Infosys’ international growth and negatively affect its business and prospects.

## Indian laws limit our ability to raise capital outside India, which could prevent us from operating our business or entering into a transaction that is in the best interests of our shareholders.

Indian foreign-exchange management laws constrain Infosys’ ability to raise capital outside India through equity or convertible debt securities. Generally, foreign investment in, or acquisition of, an Indian company does not require approval from relevant Indian government authorities, including the RBI.

However, restrictions apply to foreign investment in Indian companies in certain industrial sectors. Policy changes could restrict Infosys’ ability to raise capital. For example, limits on foreign equity ownership of Indian technology companies or pricing restrictions on ADRs or GDRs could constrain its ability to obtain additional equity investment from foreign investors.

If applied to Infosys, these restrictions could also prevent transactions such as an acquisition by a non-Indian company that might otherwise benefit Infosys and holders of its equity shares and ADSs.
