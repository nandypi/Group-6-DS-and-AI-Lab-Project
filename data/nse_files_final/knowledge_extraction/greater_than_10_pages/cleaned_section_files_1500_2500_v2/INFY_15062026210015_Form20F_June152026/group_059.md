## ADSs Issue of securities through the depository receipt mechanism

Issue of securities through the depository receipt mechanism by Indian companies is governed by the Indian Companies Act, 2013, the Companies (Issue of Global Depository Receipts) Rules, 2014 (Depository Receipts Rules), the Depository Receipts Scheme, 2014 (the “DR Scheme”), and the SEBI Master Circular for Depositories dated December 3, 2024.

The Government of India approved the DR Scheme on October 21, 2014, and it came into force on December 15, 2014. The Issue of Foreign Currency Convertible Bonds and Ordinary Shares (Through Depository Receipt Mechanism) Scheme, 1993 (the “1993 Scheme”) was consequently repealed, except to the extent relating to foreign currency convertible bonds. The DR Scheme operates in addition to other policies and facilities relating to foreign investment in Indian companies.

Under the DR Scheme, an Indian company—listed or unlisted, private or public—may issue or transfer permissible securities to a foreign depository for the issuance of a depository receipt if the company has not been specifically prohibited from accessing capital markets or dealing in securities and satisfies the prescribed conditions.

SEBI’s circular dated October 10, 2019, subsequently rescinded and replaced by the Master Circular for Depositories dated October 6, 2023, read with the Master Circular dated December 3, 2024 (the “SEBI Master Circulars”), restricted the scope of depository receipt issuance to companies incorporated in and listed on a recognized stock exchange in India. Under the SEBI Master Circulars, permissible securities are equity shares and debt securities that are in dematerialized form and rank pari passu with securities issued and listed on a recognized stock exchange.

The SEBI Master Circulars establish a detailed framework for depository receipt issuance by listed Indian companies, including eligibility requirements and issuer obligations. The circular dated October 1, 2020, on monitoring foreign holdings in depository receipts requires listed companies to appoint an Indian depository as the “Designated Depository” to monitor depository receipt limits.

The Depository Receipts (Amendment) Scheme, 2019 (the “DR Circular”), notified on October 7, 2019, amended the definition of “permissible jurisdiction” to include the International Financial Services Centre in India.

The current framework imposes additional eligibility requirements, including that an issuer must not have been declared a willful defaulter or a fugitive economic offender.

A permissible holder of depository receipts, including its beneficial owner, excludes an Indian citizen and a Non-Resident Indian (NRI). However, an NRI is not excluded as a permissible holder when depository receipts are issued pursuant to share-based employee benefit schemes implemented under the SEBI (Share Based Employee Benefits) Regulations, 2014, or pursuant to a bonus issue or rights issue.

The Company has complied with the provisions of the DR Circular and subsequent SEBI notifications applicable to the Company.

For purposes of the SEBI Master Circulars, a “permissible jurisdiction” means a jurisdiction notified by the Central Government pursuant to notification no. G.S.R. 669(E) dated September 18, 2019, in respect of sub-rule 1 of rule 9 of the Prevention of Money-Laundering (Maintenance of Records) Rules, 2005. The Central Government subsequently notified a list of permissible jurisdictions on November 28, 2019. SEBI notified the list under its circular dated November 28, 2019.

A listed company issuing depository receipts must file with the Indian stock exchanges any public disclosure made to an international stock exchange within 24 hours of filing. The issuer must also file the offer document for an initial issue of depository receipts with SEBI and the stock exchanges for their comments, if any.

A listed company issuing depository receipts must comply with applicable laws, including minimum public shareholding requirements and foreign investment holding limits under FEMA. The pricing of depository receipt issuances must be at least equivalent to the price determined for the corresponding mode of issue to domestic investors.

Under the revised framework, voting rights on the shares underlying depository receipts must be exercised by depository receipt holders. This differs from the DR Scheme, under which voting rights could be governed contractually under the deposit agreement. Under the DR Scheme, shares underlying depository receipts for which voting rights were not exercisable by depository receipt holders were not counted toward minimum public shareholding requirements.

The summary above is based on laws applicable as of March 31, 2026, and is not a complete analysis of all laws applicable to the Company and its securities or a substitute for professional legal advice.

## TAXATION

## Indian Taxation

### General

The following summary is based on the Income Tax Act, 1961, including the special tax regime under Sections 115AC and 115ACA, read with the Issue of Foreign Currency Convertible Bonds and Ordinary Shares (Through Depository Receipt Mechanism) Scheme, 1993, as amended (the “Scheme”).

The Income Tax Act is amended annually by the Finance Act of the relevant year. Some or all of the tax consequences under Sections 115AC and 115ACA may be amended or changed by future amendments.

Effective April 1, 2026, the Income Tax Act, 1961 will be replaced by the Income Tax Act, 2025. The new Act is intended to streamline tax laws, simplify language, consolidate provisions, and reduce compliance burdens.

The information is stated to be materially complete as of the date of the filing but is not a complete analysis of the individual Indian tax consequences for non-resident holders or employees acquiring, owning, or selling ADSs and equity shares. Investors and prospective investors should consult their own tax advisers regarding Indian and local tax consequences.

### Residence

Under the Income Tax Act, an individual is a resident of India during a fiscal year if the individual is in India:

- For at least 182 days during that year; or
- For at least 60 days during that year and at least 365 days during the preceding four years.

The 60-day threshold is read as 182 days:

- For an Indian citizen who leaves India during the previous year for employment outside India; or
- For an Indian citizen or person of Indian origin living abroad who visits India.

For an Indian citizen or person of Indian origin living abroad visiting India whose total income, other than income from foreign sources, exceeds Rs. 15 lakhs, the relevant period of stay is 120 days instead of 182 days. Such a person is treated as a Not Ordinarily Resident (“NOR”) if the stay in India is less than 182 days.

“Foreign source” includes income accruing or arising outside India, except income derived from a business controlled in India or a profession set up in India and income deemed to accrue or arise in India. This provision does not apply to a person resident in India.

An Indian citizen is deemed to be a resident of India if the person has income, other than income from foreign sources, exceeding Rs. 15 lakhs during the previous year and is not liable to income tax in any other country or territory by reason of domicile, residence, or a similar criterion. Such a deemed resident is treated as NOR.

A company is resident in India if it is an Indian company or if its place of effective management during the relevant year is in India. Individuals and companies that do not meet these criteria are treated as non-residents for purposes of the Income Tax Act.

## Taxation of Distributions

The Finance Act 2020 replaced Dividend Distribution Tax (“DDT”) with a classical dividend taxation system under which dividend income is taxed in the hands of shareholders at their applicable tax rates.

A company paying dividends to shareholders must withhold tax at the applicable rates under the Income Tax Act, 1961, read with the applicable tax treaty and Multilateral Instrument (“MLI”), subject to the conditions specified under the Income Tax Act, 1961.

The Finance Act 2020 amended Section 57 of the Income Tax Act, 1961, so that no deduction is allowed from dividend income except for interest expense incurred to earn the dividend. The deduction for interest expense may not exceed 20% of the dividend income included in total income for that year before any deduction under Section 57.

### Taxation of Capital Gains

The following is a brief summary of capital gains taxation for non-resident holders and resident employees relating to the sale of ADSs and equity shares received upon conversion of ADSs. The relevant provisions are contained mainly in Sections 2(42A), 45, 47(viia), 47(x), 49(2ABB), 115AC, and 115ACA of the Income Tax Act, 1961, together with the Scheme.

Shares, including shares issuable upon conversion of ADSs, held by a non-resident investor for more than 12 months are treated as long-term capital assets. Shares held for less than 12 months from the date of conversion are treated as short-term capital assets.
