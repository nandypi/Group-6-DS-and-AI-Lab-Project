## 1.3 Use of estimates and judgments

Preparation of the interim condensed standalone financial statements in conformity with Ind AS requires management to make estimates, judgments, and assumptions. These affect the application of accounting policies; reported amounts of assets, liabilities, revenues, and expenses; and disclosures of contingent assets and liabilities.

Critical accounting estimates and judgments are disclosed in Note 1.4. Estimates may change as circumstances change, and actual results may differ from those estimates. Changes in estimates are reflected in the period in which they are made, with material effects disclosed in the notes to the interim condensed standalone financial statements.

## 1.4 Critical accounting estimates and judgments

### a. Revenue recognition

The Company’s customer contracts may include multiple products and services. Revenue is recognized and measured when the contract has been approved in writing, the parties are committed to performing their obligations, and the contract is legally enforceable.

The Company identifies distinct performance obligations and allocates the transaction price to those obligations. This requires judgment regarding the deliverables and the customer’s ability to benefit independently from them.

Fixed-price maintenance revenue is recognized as follows:

- Ratably on a straight-line basis when services are performed through an indefinite number of repetitive acts over a specified period.
- Using a percentage-of-completion method when the pattern of benefits and fulfilment costs is uneven because the services are discrete rather than repetitive.

The Company uses the percentage-of-completion method for other fixed-price contracts. Progress is measured using actual efforts or costs incurred to date as a proportion of estimated total efforts or costs. Estimates of total efforts or costs are reassessed throughout the contract period.

Certain integrated services arrangements include subcontractor services or third-party vendor equipment or software. Revenue from third-party products or services is recorded net of costs when the Company acts as an agent and gross when it acts as principal. The Company assesses control of the specified goods or services by considering responsibility for fulfilment, inventory risk, pricing discretion, and other relevant factors.

Provisions for estimated losses on incomplete contracts are recorded when such losses become probable.

### b. Income taxes

The Company’s two major tax jurisdictions are India and the United States. The Company also files tax returns in other overseas jurisdictions.

Significant judgment is required in determining income tax provisions, including amounts expected to be paid or recovered for uncertain tax positions.

When assessing the realizability of deferred income tax assets, management considers scheduled reversals of deferred income tax liabilities, projected future taxable income, and tax-planning strategies. The realizable amount could be reduced if estimates of future taxable income during the carry-forward period are reduced. Refer to Note 2.16.

### c. Property, plant and equipment

Property, plant and equipment represent a significant proportion of the Company’s asset base. Depreciation depends on estimates of expected useful lives and residual values. Management determines these estimates when assets are acquired and reviews them periodically, including at each financial year-end.

The estimates are based on historical experience with similar assets and anticipated future events, including changes in technology. Refer to Note 2.1.

## 2. Notes to the Interim Condensed Standalone Financial Statements

## 2.1 PROPERTY, PLANT AND EQUIPMENT

### Accounting Policy

Property, plant and equipment are stated at cost less accumulated depreciation and impairment, if any. Costs directly attributable to acquisition are capitalized until the assets are ready for use as intended by management.

Property, plant and equipment are depreciated over their estimated useful lives using the straight-line method.

The estimated useful lives of assets are as follows:

| Asset | Estimated useful life |
|---|---|
| Building (1) | 22–25 years |
| Plant and machinery (1) | 5 years |
| Office equipment | 5 years |
| Computer equipment (1) | 3–5 years |
| Furniture and fixtures (1) | 5 years |
| Vehicles (1) | 5 years |
| Leasehold improvements | Lower of useful life of the asset or lease term |

(1) Based on technical evaluation, management believes that the useful lives stated above best represent the period over which it expects to use these assets. Accordingly, the useful lives for these assets differ from those prescribed under Part C of Schedule II of the Companies Act, 2013.

Depreciation methods, useful lives, and residual values are reviewed periodically, including at each financial year-end.

Advances paid toward the acquisition of property, plant and equipment that remain outstanding at each Balance Sheet date are classified as capital advances under other non-current assets. The cost of assets not ready for use at that date is disclosed as capital work-in-progress.

Subsequent expenditure relating to property, plant and equipment is capitalized only when future economic benefits are probable and the cost can be measured reliably. The cost and related accumulated depreciation are removed from the financial statements upon sale or retirement of the asset.

### Impairment

Property, plant and equipment are evaluated for recoverability whenever events or changes in circumstances indicate that their carrying amounts may not be recoverable.

The recoverable amount, being the higher of fair value less costs to sell and value in use, is determined on an individual asset basis unless the asset does not generate cash flows that are largely independent of those from other assets. In such cases, recoverable amount is determined for the Cash Generating Unit (CGU) to which the asset belongs.

An impairment loss recognized in the interim condensed Statement of Profit and Loss is measured as the excess of the asset’s carrying value over its estimated recoverable amount. An impairment loss is reversed when there is a change in the estimates used to determine recoverable amount, subject to the asset’s revised carrying amount not exceeding the amount that would have been determined, net of accumulated depreciation, had no impairment loss been recognized in prior years.
